Part one

Why Bayesian optimization

What it is, what it costs, and how it compares with the alternatives you already know.

1. Why BO 2. CFD loop 3. Overview 4. Building blocks 5. Bayes 6. Acquisition 7. Objectives 8. Fidelity 9. Conclusions

Efficient global optimization of expensive, derivative-free, multi-modal functions.

That single sentence is the whole promise. Every property below follows from it — and every one of them matters in CFD, where one function evaluation is a simulation.

What Bayesian optimization buys you

  • Sample-efficient — finds a global optimum in roughly 100 evaluations.
  • Integrates expert knowledge rigorously, through the prior and the kernel.
  • Computes variance — uncertainty quantification comes free with the model.
  • Supports multi-objective, multi-fidelity and constrained optimization.
  • Handles real, integer and categorical (integer-encoded) components of x.
  • Proven at scale in machine learning — hyperparameter tuning and AutoML, including AlphaFold and AlphaGo.

Scope: f : ℝd → ℝ with d ≲ 60. We can parameterise a fluid-dynamics simulation or an experiment in sixty search dimensions.

Compared with gradient-based optimization

What it costs you

  • can get stuck in a local optimum;
  • requires deriving and implementing an adjoint problem — per objective, per model;
  • on complex problems, density- and gradient-based topology optimization often yields disconnected topology, or shapes that cannot be manufactured.

The adjoint is not free labour. Every change to the physics — a new turbulence model, a new boundary condition — is a new derivation.

Where an adjoint is available and the problem is unimodal, it wins on cost. BO is for the case where it is not.

Compared with evolutionary algorithms

10³
Evaluations, empirically. An order of magnitude more than BO needs — decisive when each one is a simulation.
—
Heuristic, with no measure of uncertainty. You get a best-so-far, not a model you can interrogate.
d < 200
But they scale further in dimension. Above roughly 60 parameters, this is the honest choice.

The trade is simple: BO spends thinking to save evaluations. That is the right trade exactly when evaluations are simulations.

Two CFD applications

Chip cooling

A parameterised cooling geometry. Objectives in tension: heat removed against pressure loss. The result is a Pareto front, sampled and then confirmed, from which an engineer picks against a pump curve.

Passive chaotic micromixing

No moving parts: the walls must stretch and fold the interface. Objectives in tension: mixing quality against pressure loss.

Both are CAD-in-the-loop: the design variables are geometry, so every proposal must be built, meshed and solved before it can be scored.

Constraining the parametric CAD

The optimiser will propose the corners of your box. Every corner must be a shape that can be built, meshed and solved.

Why it matters

An unconstrained box contains slivers, self-intersections and features below the mesh resolution. Each one costs a wasted CFD run to discover — or worse, produces a number that looks fine.

How to do it

Express dependent parameters as ratios into admissible intervals whose endpoints depend on the other parameters. The optimiser then searches a plain unit box, and feasibility is structural rather than checked after the fact.

Two kinds of failure, and only one of them is a result

A result

The design was built, meshed and solved — and it diverged, or failed a quality gate, or produced a flow you would never accept. Record it. That region of the design space really is bad, and the model should know.

Not a result

A missing binary, an unbound path, a rejected sbatch, an out-of-memory, a stale library. The design was fine; the machinery failed. Recorded as a failure it teaches the model that a perfectly good region is hopeless — and nothing in the output ever says otherwise.

The exit code is the same in both cases. Nothing downstream can tell them apart unless the workflow was built to.

The discriminator that works: physics failures scatter; a broken tool fails identically every time. Have every step record its own name when it fails, probe the environment before the first design is touched, and stop the campaign when several designs in a row die in the same step.

What that costs, exactly

Nothing crashed. The campaign runs to completion, reports a Pareto front, and is wrong — because a failed run was written back as a failed design.

Same six designs and the same kernel in both panels; only two recorded values differ. The penalty value used here is -2.5, which is the sort of number a failure handler writes so the optimiser "avoids that region". It works — that is the problem.

Everything converged. Everything was wrong.

In one micromixer optimization campaign, the CAD classified a boundary face as an inlet by the direction it pointed rather than where it was. Every obstacle face that happened to face upstream became an inlet, so the solver injected fluid through the middle of the device.

What the diagnostics said

Nothing. Every solve converged. checkMesh reported Mesh OK. Residuals fell. The Pareto fronts looked plausible, and were smooth.

What was actually true

The inlet area was 2.85 × H instead of 1 × H. 28 designs — an entire campaign — had to be discarded and could not be pooled with the corrected ones.

Only a test that knows where an inlet is supposed to be could have caught this. No exit code, no residual and no mesh check ever could.
This is the argument for writing the physical, top-level test first: the straight channel against its analytic pressure drop. It is the cheapest test in the campaign and the only one that would have failed here.
Part three

Bayesian optimization at a glance

The goal, the two building blocks, and the loop — before the mathematics.

1. Why BO 2. CFD loop 3. Overview 4. Building blocks 5. Bayes 6. Acquisition 7. Objectives 8. Fidelity 9. Conclusions

The goal

x* = argmaxx ∈ X f(x),   x ∈ Id,  d ≲ 20–60

f is an expensive black box: no formula, no gradient, and each evaluation is a simulation.

Bayesian optimization models f with a cheap probabilistic surrogate — a Gaussian process (GP) — and optimises that instead, using it to decide where the next expensive evaluation should go.

Two building blocks, one loop

The Gaussian process

A probabilistic surrogate for the expensive function. It supplies closed-form, inexpensive mean μ(x) and variance σ²(x).

Globally optimising f is replaced by globally optimising the GP.

The acquisition function

Optimising μ alone would only exploit. Including σ² lets us explore where the GP is uncertain.

The acquisition function combines the two into one number to maximise.

The Bayesian optimization loop

Before the loop: evaluate f at n0 space-filling points (Sobol or Latin hypercube) to get D = {(xi, yi)}. The loop cannot start from nothing — a GP with no data proposes only the corners of the box.

1
Fit the GP to D: maximise the log marginal likelihood over θ = (ℓ, σf, σn).
2
Condition on D to get the posterior mean μ′(x) and standard deviation σ′(x).
3
Maximise the acquisition, xnew = argmaxx α(x; μ′, σ′). Cheap, so: global, many restarts.
4
Evaluate the expensive f at xnew — one design, or q of them in parallel.
5
AugmentD ← D ∪ {(xnew, ynew)}, and go to 1.

Stop on the budget you can afford, or when maxx α(x) < ε — the model itself reporting that nothing left is worth buying. Do not stop because the best value has not improved for a few iterations: BO spends those iterations exploring on purpose, and that test would halt it exactly when it is doing its job.

Part four

Building blocks

Gaussian distribution → multivariate Gaussian → Gaussian process. Each step adds exactly one idea.

1. Why BO 2. CFD loop 3. Overview 4. Building blocks 5. Bayes 6. Acquisition 7. Objectives 8. Fidelity 9. Conclusions

The Gaussian distribution

p(x) = (2πσ²)−1/2 exp( −(x−μ)² / 2σ² )

μ — the mean, ∫ x p(x) dx

σ — the standard deviation, the spread

σ² — the variance

From one variable to a random vector

Take n Gaussian variables, yi ~ N(μi, σi), and collect them:

y = ( y1 = f1(x1), … , yn = fn(xn) )T

Assume for now they are independent. Then the joint density is just the product of the individual ones.

p(y) = (2πσ²)−1 exp( −[ (y1−μ)² + (y2−μ)² ] / 2σ² )

Written here for n = 2 with σ1 = σ2 = σ and μ1 = μ2 = μ, to keep the algebra visible.

What do the iso-probability contours look like?

Set p(y) = λ and take the logarithm:

(y1−μ)² + (y2−μ)² = 2σ² log( 1 / 2πσλ )

The right-hand side is a constant. So this is the equation of a circle.

Independence ⇒ a diagonal covariance matrix ⇒ circular contours. No direction in the plane is special.

The independent multivariate Gaussian, in vector form

Collecting the normalisation constants and combining the exponents gives

p(y) = (2π)−n/2 |Σ|−1/2 exp( −½ (y − μ)T Σ−1 (y − μ) )

The quadratic form −½ (y − μ)T Σ−1 (y − μ) is the squared Mahalanobis distance between the random vector and the mean — distance measured in units of the distribution's own spread.

So far Σ is diagonal. Everything interesting happens when it is not.

Adding dependence: the simplest possible coupling

Dependence means off-diagonal entries in Σ. The simplest way to create them is a linear transformation — an affine map:

yd = A yi + b   ⇔    yi = A−1(yd − b)

We invert it because we want to use the product rule, which only holds for the independent vector.

Changing variables in a density costs a Jacobian factor:

p(yd) = pyi( A−1(yd − b) ) · |det A−1|

The dependent multivariate Gaussian

Substituting and tidying, with

m := Aμ + b,   Σd = A Σi AT

p(yd) = (2π)−n/2|Σd|−1/2 exp( −½ (yd−m)T Σd−1 (yd−m) )

Same shape as before — only Σ has changed.

Three properties of Σ that we will need

1
Symmetric. ΣdT = (A Σi AT)T = A Σi AT = Σd.
2
Positive definite. Σi is diagonal and positive definite; if A is invertible, so is Σd.
3
Invertible. Non-negotiable — every expression we are heading for contains Σd−1.

These become the load-bearing conditions the moment we replace the numbers in Σ with the values of a kernel function. That is the next step, and it is the whole idea of a Gaussian process.

The Gaussian process

Let X be the N parameter vectors we have sampled. Replace the entries of Σ by a covariance function k:

f(x) ~ N( μ(x), K(X) ),   K(X)ij = k(xi, xj)

A Gaussian process is a finite set of random variables drawn from a dependent multivariate Gaussian — one whose covariance matrix is generated by a kernel rather than tabulated.

Because k must produce a symmetric, positive-definite K, you cannot choose any function k.

A prior over functions, not over numbers

Every coloured line is one function drawn from the prior. Before any data, the GP says: the truth is some function like these — mean zero, and smooth on the length scale ℓ set by the kernel.

Drawn by Cholesky-factorising the kernel matrix over the grid and multiplying by standard normals — the same construction as the maths on the previous slides, run for real.

One number decides how much f is allowed to wiggle

Same five random draws in all three panels. Only ℓ changed — and with it, everything the model is prepared to believe about the parts of f it has not seen.

The coloured curves correspond one-to-one across the panels: identical standard normals, different Cholesky factor. This is the place to say that one length scale per dimension is exactly what ARD will later exploit.

What you actually choose

  • A GP is a surrogate model that assumes the system is random.
  • Not merely random — specifically Gaussian. That may not be the best assumption for a given problem; in CFD, practice shows it works.
  • As the engineer, you choose the covariance kernel and its parameters.
Choosing the kernel is the modelling step. It encodes what you believe about smoothness, length scales, and which parameters matter.

Same five simulations, three beliefs, three different answers

The right-hand panel is the dangerous one: the band is narrow exactly where the mean is most wrong. An over-smooth prior does not report its own misspecification.

Grey dashed is the hidden truth; the black dots are the same five observations in all three panels. Nothing changed but one kernel parameter. This is the motivation for the next slide: you do not want to be choosing this number by eye.

Fitting the GP to data: maximum likelihood

The GP is a parametric density GP(y, θ), where θ collects the kernel parameters and the noise σ. Fitting means

θ* = argmaxθ GP(y, θ)

Because the GP is a multivariate Gaussian, efficient closed-form expressions for ∂θᵢGP exist. Since it involves exponentials, we maximise the log marginal likelihood:

log p(y | θ) = −½ yT (Kθ + σ²I)−1 y − ½ log|Kθ + σ²I| − (n/2) log 2π

The three terms trade off against each other: data fit, a complexity penalty, and a constant. The middle term is why maximum likelihood does not simply drive the length scales to zero.

What the fit gives you — and what it does not

Maximum likelihood fits θ, not f. It chooses which family of functions the model finds plausible. It does not make the model pass through a single data point — that is the next section's job.

Ten samples here, not the five used elsewhere, and deliberately: with five the profile is nearly flat and the optimiser runs to the lower bound on the length scale, declaring the data uncorrelated noise. Hyperparameters need data too. In practice you bound the length scales or put priors on them precisely because early in a campaign there is not enough data to identify them — worth saying out loud, because it is the state every CFD campaign starts in.
Part five

Bayes, and conditioning the GP

How observing data turns the prior over functions into a posterior.

1. Why BO 2. CFD loop 3. Overview 4. Building blocks 5. Bayes 6. Acquisition 7. Objectives 8. Fidelity 9. Conclusions

John is quiet, nerdy, and loves solving logic puzzles.

Is John more likely to be a mathematician or a librarian?

The description is not the whole story

Statistically there are far more librarians than mathematicians.

Even if a mathematician matches that description better, the sheer base rate of librarians can outweigh it. Which way the answer falls depends entirely on the ratio of the two populations — a number the description never mentions.

This is the mistake Bayes' theorem exists to prevent: judging by fit to the evidence while ignoring how much space the hypothesis occupied to begin with.

Put numbers on it

Suppose there are 20 librarians for every mathematician, and suppose the description fits 8 in 10 mathematicians but only 1 in 10 librarians. In odds form, Bayes is one multiplication:

prior odds
1 : 20
how many of each exist
×
likelihood ratio
8 : 1
how well each explains the description
=
posterior odds
1 : 2.5
P(mathematician | description) = 0.29

The description fits a mathematician eight times better — and John is still more than twice as likely to be a librarian.

The odds form is worth writing out: posterior odds = prior odds x likelihood ratio, with no normalising constant to carry. Evidence multiplies the prior, it does not replace it — which is exactly what the GP posterior does on the next slides, pulling the prior around rather than overwriting it.

Bayes' theorem

Given an observation, what is the probability that the hypothesis is true?

P(H | D) = P(D | H) P(H) / [ P(D | H) P(H) + P(D | ¬H) P(¬H) ]

P(H)

the prior — how plausible H was before we looked

P(D | H)

the likelihood — how well H explains what we saw

P(H | D)

the posterior — what to believe now

The denominator is just the total probability of seeing D at all, by either route. The posterior is a ratio of areas.

Marginalising: integrating out what you did not ask about

A joint density carries every variable at once. Usually you want a statement about one of them, with the rest allowed to be anything they like. That is a marginal:

p(y1) = ∫ p(y1, y2) dy2

Why it appears here

Bayes' denominator is a marginal — p(D) is the joint p(D, H) summed over every hypothesis. It is the total probability of seeing the data by any route, which is what turns the numerator into a proper probability.

Why it usually hurts

That integral is over every unobserved variable. In a general model it is the expensive, often intractable step — the reason large parts of Bayesian inference are about approximating it.

For Gaussians the integral has a closed form, and it is almost insultingly cheap.

Two operations on the same joint

Conditioning is what a measurement does. The mean moves toward what was observed and the variance falls — and those two sentences are the whole content of μ′ and σ′.

Same bivariate Gaussian in both panels, correlation 0.8. Left: the orange verticals are the columns being summed. Right: the orange horizontal is the observation, and the curve above it is that one slice, renormalised. Conditional variance is 1 - rho^2 = 0.36, so sd = 0.6 — and note it does not depend on WHICH value y2 took, only that it was observed. That is why the GP band collapses at a sample regardless of the value measured there.

Why a Gaussian makes this free

Marginalising = deleting

Take a jointly Gaussian vector, drop the components you do not want, and the rest is still Gaussian — with the mean and covariance you get by striking out the corresponding rows and columns. No integral is performed.

N(μ, Σ) → N(μA, ΣAA)

What that buys a GP

A GP is a distribution over functions — formally, over uncountably many random variables. You can only ever hold a finite number of them in a computer.

The marginalisation property says you do not have to: any finite set of points is already the exact marginal of the infinite object. The N designs you simulated are the whole model, as far as any computation is concerned.

This is the definition of a Gaussian process: a collection of random variables, any finite subset of which is jointly Gaussian.

Conditioning a GP is Bayes in a continuous setting

GP(y′ | y) = GP(y′) GP(y | y′) / GP(y)

The recipe

  1. The observed y and the wanted y′ come from the same GP, so write their joint as one Gaussian with a block covariance: K(X,X) + σ²I, K(X,x′) and k(x′,x′).

  2. The denominator GP(y) is that joint marginalised over y′ — the evidence, which by the previous slide costs nothing to write down.

  3. Divide, cancel, apply the block-matrix inverse. The exponent stays quadratic, so the result is another Gaussian — and you read its mean and covariance straight off.

The posterior of a Gaussian conditioned on Gaussian data is again Gaussian. That closure is the reason any of this is cheap.

The two formulas everything rests on

μ′(x′) = μ(x′) + k(x′,X)T (K(X) + σ²I)−1 ( y − μ(X) )

(σ′)²(x′) = k(x′,x′) − k(x′,X)T (K(X) + σ²I)−1 k(x′,X)

μ′ — the updated mean: it interpolates the data.

(σ′)² — the updated variance: it collapses at the data and grows away from it.

K is N × N where N is the number of simulations you have run. For a CFD campaign, N ≤ 100 — a solve measured in microseconds.

Turning a mean and a variance into a decision about where to spend the next simulation.

Upper confidence bound

αUCB(x) = μ′(x) + κ σ′(x),   κ ∈ ℝ⁺

μ′ — exploit

Go where the posterior already predicts a good value.

σ′ — explore

Go where the posterior is uncertain.

κ — the dial

How much exploration you are willing to pay for.

UCB is the simplest useful acquisition. Expected improvement, knowledge gradient and the hypervolume-based multi-objective forms all make the same trade with more machinery.

Posterior: the mean interpolates, the variance collapses

An optimization inside the optimization

Maximising α(x) is itself a global optimization problem. Why is that affordable?

Because the objective changed

α is built from μ′ and σ′ — two matrix products. Evaluating it a million times costs less than one time step of the simulation it is standing in for.

So it may be greedy

Multi-start, many restarts, dense raw sampling. Exactly the brute force you could never afford on f itself.

Then: sample f at the maximiser, add the point, refit, and go round again. The expensive function is called once per iteration.

The loop, running

Forrester function, kappa = 2, one point added per panel. The dashed vertical is the argmax of the acquisition — the design the loop is about to simulate. Panel two spends a simulation on a point that turns out to be bad: that is the cost of exploration, and the next slide is what it buys.

What the exploration was for

κ = 0 never leaves the first hill it climbs. With no reward for uncertainty the acquisition just maximises the mean, and the mean is only informative where data already exists. It re-proposes the same design forever.

Random search does not get stuck — it is only slow. Averaged over 40 runs it is still short of the optimum after 18 evaluations, which BO reached on its eighth.

Same function, same three starting points, same budget; only the strategy differs. This is the concrete version of the "roughly 100 evaluations" claim from part one. Say plainly that this is a one-dimensional toy and that the gap widens with dimension — but that the kappa = 0 failure mode is real and does show up in production campaigns.
Part seven

Many objectives at once

Where the objectives come from, why you should not add them up, and how to choose in the end.

1. Why BO 2. CFD loop 3. Overview 4. Building blocks 5. Bayes 6. Acquisition 7. Objectives 8. Fidelity 9. Conclusions

Two objectives, and no single answer

Almost nothing in CFD has one objective. Pressure drop and mixing. Lift and drag. Heat transfer and pumping power. Efficiency and cavitation margin. They conflict, so “the optimum” is not a design — it is a set.

Dominance

Design a dominates b if it is at least as good in every objective and strictly better in at least one. Then b is simply never worth building.

The Pareto front

The designs that nothing dominates. Moving along it, you cannot improve one objective without giving up another — which is the trade-off, made explicit and measured rather than assumed.

Reporting a front instead of a winner is the honest output. It also defers the value judgement until you can see what it costs.

An objective is one number squeezed out of a whole field

Reduce

Δp from a patch-averaged pressure. Mixing from a variance integral over the outlet. Drag from a force integral. One function object, one number, once per design.

Normalise

Divide by a matched baseline. A GP given one objective in pascals and another in [0, 1] is a GP fitting the pascals.

Sign, once

Negate what you minimise in exactly one place. And give each objective its own noise: solver tolerance is not the same for a pressure as for a scalar variance.

Getting the objective wrong is quieter, and far more expensive, than getting the optimiser wrong.
One GP per objective, fitted independently, is the usual choice and usually enough. A multi-output GP that shares information across objectives helps only when they genuinely share structure and you have the data to identify it — which early in a campaign you do not.

A constraint is not an objective.

An objective is traded off. A constraint decides whether a design exists. Mesh quality, a Reynolds bound, a wall clearance, manufacturability — not one of them is a quantity you would accept less of in exchange for more mixing.

So do not give it a weight

What goes wrong

Add a constraint as another weighted term and you have priced it. The optimiser will then buy a violation whenever the other objective pays enough — and report that design as a success.

What to do instead

Model feasibility separately: a second GP over “did this design survive?”, and multiply the acquisition by the probability that it will. Infeasible regions become cheap to avoid rather than expensive to buy.

Feasible or not is a different question from good or bad, and it needs its own model.

The tempting shortcut: add them up

minimise   w1 f1(x) + w2 f2(x)

You must price it first

The weights are the trade-off — the very thing the campaign was meant to measure. You are asked to choose them before you have seen it.

One weighting, one design

A single weight vector returns a single point. Recovering a front means running the whole campaign again, once per weighting.

And there is a third objection, worse than both.

No weighting at all reaches a concave front

Sweep the weights and the chosen design jumps across the gap. Every design on the red arc is a perfectly good, non-dominated compromise — and no weighted sum will ever select one.

Computed here: the front is sampled, its lower convex hull taken, and the longest hull edge identifies the concave stretch. The critical weight and the two jump endpoints are read off that geometry, not typed in. ε-constraint methods do reach concave regions — "minimise f1 subject to f2 ≤ ε" — but they still need you to pick ε, and still cost one campaign per ε.

Hypervolume: one number for a whole front

A front cannot be ranked against another front without a scalar — and it has to be a scalar of the set, not of any one design. The hypervolume is the area the front dominates with respect to a declared reference point. It rewards a front for being good, for being wide, and for being evenly covered, all at once, which is why it became the standard.

The reference point is a modelling choice, not a detail: it must be dominated by everything you would accept, because designs worse than it contribute nothing and become invisible to the optimiser. Place it a little beyond the worst tolerable value of each objective. Placing it deliberately close on one axis is also how you tell the optimiser to concentrate there.

The acquisition for a front

α(x) = E[ HV(P ∪ f(x)) − HV(P) ]

Expected hypervolume improvement. How much area this candidate is expected to add to the front — averaged over everything the GP still does not know about it.

It is expected improvement, with “improvement” redefined from a better number to a better front.

q, N, Log — three fixes that make it usable

q — jointly

Propose q designs at once and score them as a set, so two candidates that would add the same area are not both bought. The difference between a parallel campaign and a merely simultaneous one.

N — noisy

The front you already have is itself uncertain. Integrate over the posterior of the evaluated points rather than treating them as exact — unavoidable when the objectives are solver output.

Log

Improvements collapse toward zero late in a campaign, and the gradients vanish with them. Optimising the log keeps the surface climbable — the difference between correct and usable.

Together: qLogNEHVI.

Exact hypervolume improvement costs time exponential in the number of objectives. Two or three are comfortable, four is work; beyond that you are using an approximation, and should know that you are.

Lineage worth naming: qEHVI (Daulton et al. 2020) made it differentiable and parallel, qNEHVI (2021) handled observation noise, and the LogEI family (Ament et al. 2023) fixed the numerical vanishing.

Choosing a design from the front

The optimiser returns a set. Picking one member is a decision, not a computation — and the whole point of deferring it was to make it with the trade-off in front of you.

Ways to pick

  • Apply the real constraint. “Best mixing with Δp under the pump budget” reads straight off the front — usually the honest question.
  • The knee. Maximum curvature: where a further gain starts costing disproportionately. Defensible when nothing else discriminates.
  • Weights, afterwards. Score the finished front under many weightings. Free, unlike scalarising the search, and it shows how sensitive the choice is.
  • Nearest to a target. Name the values you want; take the closest achievable point.

If you already know what you want

Say so before the campaign rather than after. Placing the hypervolume reference point to emphasise a region, or steering with reference vectors, concentrates the simulations where you care instead of buying a uniform front you will use a tenth of.

And a front is a finite set of simulated designs, not a curve. To take a design “between” two front points you must simulate it: interpolating in objective space gives you a number, not a geometry.

Part eight

Cheaper evaluations

Not every simulation has to be the expensive one.

1. Why BO 2. CFD loop 3. Overview 4. Building blocks 5. Bayes 6. Acquisition 7. Objectives 8. Fidelity 9. Conclusions

Fidelity is a knob you already have

Before you spend €50 000 on GPUs and train a neural network to stand in for your solver: run your solver on a coarser mesh.

Every CFD study already contains a cheaper version of itself, and it is usually sitting in the case directory:

Coarser mesh

Halving the cell count per direction is 8× fewer cells in 3-D, and the larger stable time step multiplies that again.

Shorter or looser

Stop at a slacker residual, average over fewer flow-through times, or take a steady solve where you would take a transient one.

Simpler physics

RANS instead of LES, 2-D instead of 3-D, an incompressible solve where the Mach number barely justifies a compressible one.

The arithmetic that decides it: if the cheap level is 10× cheaper, one expensive run buys ten cheap ones. Multi-fidelity is worth building only when that ratio is large and the cheap level tells you something about the expensive one.

Biased, not noisy — and still worth having

The cheap model is useful for its shape, not its values. Optimise it directly and you get the wrong answer with confidence; model the difference between the levels and you get most of the expensive model for a fraction of the price.

The standard multi-fidelity Forrester pair, in this deck's scaling. Right panel is recursive co-kriging: fit the cheap model densely, regress the four expensive points onto it (rho = 0.97 here), and give the leftover discrepancy its own GP with a long length scale. RMSE 0.79 -> 0.34, and the predicted argmax moves from 0.57 to 0.71 against a truth of 0.76.

Model the gap, not the levels

fhigh(x) = ρ flow(x) + δ(x)

The cheap level carries the shape

Fit it densely — you can afford to. It supplies almost all of the structure.

A second GP carries δ

Only the discrepancy needs expensive data, and δ is usually far smoother than f itself. That is the entire saving.

The modern equivalent is one GP over the pair (x, s), with s the fidelity level and a kernel that multiplies a spatial part by a fidelity part — so the correlation between levels is learned rather than assumed.

The acquisition has to know the price

An acquisition that only asks “where do I learn the most?” picks the expensive level every single time — because it always is the most informative.

Divide by cost

Information per euro of compute, not information. Knowledge-gradient and entropy-search forms take this naturally, since both already measure information per evaluation.

Fidelity becomes a search dimension

Add s to the space, attach a cost model, and the optimiser chooses where and at what fidelity. In BoTorch: qMultiFidelityKnowledgeGradient.

The fidelity stops being your decision and becomes part of the optimiser's.

Measure the correlation before you build any of this

Run a handful of designs at both levels and compute the rank correlation. That is a cheap experiment, and it decides whether the rest is worth doing.

It pays when

  • the cheap level ranks designs roughly as the expensive one does — agreement in value matters far less;
  • the cost ratio is genuinely 5–10× or better;
  • the discrepancy is smooth, so a few expensive runs pin it down.

It does not pay when

  • the coarse mesh is qualitatively different — separation not captured, a recirculation missing, transition in the wrong place;
  • a 2-D proxy for a 3-D device, which is different physics rather than cheaper physics;
  • δ turns out to be as hard to learn as f, in which case you have added a model and bought nothing.

A cheap level that ranks designs wrongly is worse than no cheap level: it spends your expensive budget confirming the wrong candidates.

What to take away

BO is for expensive, derivative-free, multi-modal problems — which is what a CAD-in-the-loop CFD study is.
The GP is a prior over functions, made concrete by a kernel; conditioning it on data is Bayes' theorem in a continuous setting.
The posterior mean and variance are closed-form and cheap, which is what makes an inner global optimization affordable.
The acquisition function is where exploration is bought, and κ is the price you set.
Constraining the CAD is not housekeeping — it is what stops the optimiser spending simulations on shapes that cannot exist.
Real problems have several objectives, so the answer is a Pareto front. Do not add them up — a weighted sum prices a trade-off you have not measured yet, and cannot reach a concave front at all.

Bayesian optimization does not make a simulation cheaper.
It makes each one count.

Everything in this deck — the Gaussian, the kernel, the conditioning, the acquisition — exists to answer one question: given what the simulations so far have told us, which single design is worth running next?
