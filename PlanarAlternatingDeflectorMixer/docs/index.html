Part one

The problem

What the device is, which numbers we are allowed to change, and what exactly counts as “better”.

A passive micromixer: no moving parts, only geometry

A flat channel, 1 mm tall and 24 mm long, carrying two co-flowing streams. One enters with scalar T=1, the other with T=0. We want them mixed by the outlet.

There is no stirrer. The only thing we can design is the shape of the walls.

  • five identical unit cells, 4 mm each;
  • a centre baffle that splits the stream and re-merges it;
  • a pair of cosine deflectors per cell, one on each wall, of different amplitude — hence “alternating”.
FixedValue
Channel height H1 mm
Total length L24 mm
Unit cells N5
Cell length4 mm
Fluidwater, ν = 10⁻⁶ m²/s
Mean velocity0.01 m/s
Diffusivity D10⁻⁹ m²/s
Geometry generator: FlowCase/alternating_deflector_cad.py. Operating point: research_config.yaml.

Why this is hard: laminar flow, and diffusion that will not help

Re = 10
Inertia is negligible. The flow is steady and laminar — there is no turbulence to do the mixing for us.
Sc = 1000
Momentum diffuses a thousand times faster than the scalar does.
Pe = 10⁴
Over the residence time, molecular diffusion crosses only a sliver of the channel.

So mixing has to come from geometry. The walls must stretch and fold the material interface until diffusion has a short enough distance left to finish the job. Every bit of that stretching costs pressure.

That tension — interface area against pumping cost — is the entire optimisation problem.

Pe = Re·Sc = 10 000. This is why a straight channel mixes essentially not at all over 24 mm.

Six numbers we are allowed to change

BO coordinateBoundsWhat it does to the device
aweak0 – 0.12 Hamplitude of the shallow wall deflector
astrong,ratio0 – 1maps into astrong ∈ [max(0.12, aweak+0.04), 0.35] — the asymmetry that drives transverse flow
ts0.04 – 0.15 Hcentre-baffle thickness in the split section
tm,ratio0 – 1maps into the mesh-safe merge-baffle thickness interval
Lc0.40 – 2.40 Hlength over which the two deflectors interact
Ls,ratio0 – 1splits the remaining cell length between split and merge sections

Three of the six are ratios, not lengths. They map into intervals whose endpoints depend on the other parameters, so the optimiser searches a plain unit box and every corner of that box is a geometry that can actually be built and meshed.

bo_to_geo() in bayes_optimize_sequential.py. k is pinned to 0, so all five cells share one amplitude pair.

Two objectives, both minimised, both measured not modelled

Pumping cost

Jdp(θ) = Δp(θ) / Δpstraight

Normalised by the validated straight channel at the same operating point, so it reads as “how many times more expensive to pump than an empty pipe”.

Δpstraight = 2.874 Pa, within 0.039 % of the analytic parallel-plate solution 12νUL/H².

Remaining segregation

Jmix(θ) = Is,flux = σ²T,flux ⁄ 0.25

Flux-weighted variance of the outlet scalar, normalised by the fully segregated value. 0 = perfectly mixed, 1 = untouched.

Reported as the mixing index 1 − √Is,flux, where 1 is perfect.

Flux weighting matters: an area average would credit mixing in slow corners that barely any fluid passes through.

Both come from custom OpenFOAM function objects — pressureDrop and patchMixingQuality in src/functionObjects.

Constraints: what the optimiser is not allowed to propose

Manufacturable and meshable

  • minimum feature ≥ 0.01 H (10 µm) — the wall-refinement cell size;
  • merge baffle ≥ 2 fine cells;
  • peak-to-peak gap ≥ 4 fine cells;
  • split-to-merge step ≥ 1 fine cell.

Enforced by interval arithmetic before CAD runs, so no CFD time is spent on a sliver.

Admissible as evidence

  • every inlet/outlet face exactly on x = 0 / x = L;
  • checkMesh must report Mesh OK;
  • flow rate within 3 % of U·A;
  • mass imbalance < 10⁻⁵;
  • outlet metric stationary, not merely residual-converged.

Fail any of these and no objective is written at all.

Reference budget: Δp ≤ 20 Pa · useful mixing index ≥ 0.60 — declared before any data was taken.
Predeclaring the gate is what makes the later no-go a result rather than a rationalisation.
Part two

Bayesian optimization

From Bayes’ theorem, to a belief about a whole function, to knowing which simulation to run next.

Is John a librarian or a mathematician?

“John is quiet, tidy, and loves patterns and order. Is John more likely to be a librarian or a mathematician?”

Almost everyone says librarian. The description sounds like one.

But there are perhaps 20× as many mathematicians as librarians in the population we drew John from. Even if the description fits a librarian four times better, mathematicians still win — by a factor of five.

P(H | D) = P(D | H) · P(H) / P(D)

  • P(H) — the prior: how common is the hypothesis before we look;
  • P(D|H) — the likelihood: how well the hypothesis explains what we saw;
  • P(H|D) — the posterior: what we should now believe.

The intuition that fails here is exactly the one that fails in optimisation: we judge by fit to the evidence and forget how much space the hypothesis occupied to begin with.

Kahneman & Tversky's base-rate neglect, transplanted. The point is that a posterior is an area ratio, which the next slide makes literal.

Bayes’ theorem is an area ratio

The whole square is 1. The likelihood carves a slice out of each column; the posterior is the blue share of the coloured region. Everything later is this picture with continuous variables.

Now put a prior on a whole function

Our unknown is not a coin bias. It is the map

θ ↦ J(θ)

from six geometry numbers to an objective — and every single evaluation costs a CFD run.

A Gaussian process is a prior over functions instead of over numbers.

In one sentence

A GP says: I do not know the function, but I believe similar designs give similar answers — and it makes “similar” precise with a kernel.

Feed it the runs you have. Bayes’ rule turns the prior into a posterior. Because everything is Gaussian, that posterior is available in closed form.

Matérn-5/2 with ARD: one length scale per parameter, so the model learns which of the six actually matter.

ARD length scales are a free sensitivity analysis — see part three.

What you get back is a mean and an error bar, everywhere

Both panels are computed live from a squared-exponential kernel and an exact posterior — the mean interpolates the five points and the variance collapses there. Nothing is drawn by hand.

The two closed forms that make this cheap

μ(θ) = k(θ)ᵀ K⁻¹ y

σ²(θ) = k(θ,θ) − k(θ)ᵀ K⁻¹ k(θ)

K is n×n where n is the number of simulations you have run. For us n ≤ 100.

Inverting a 100×100 matrix is free. Evaluating μ and σ at a million candidate designs is free. One CFD run is ten minutes.

That asymmetry is the whole reason the method works — and it is why the surrogate happily lives on a login node while the simulations go to the queue.

σ(θ) is not decoration. It is the model telling you where it is guessing, which is precisely where a new run is worth paying for.

Combining them: exploitation + exploration in one number

αUCB(θ) = μ(θ) + κ · σ(θ)

μ — exploit

Go where the model already predicts a good answer. On its own this gets stuck in the first decent basin it finds.

σ — explore

Go where the model is ignorant. On its own this samples the corners forever and never refines anything.

κ — the dial

How many error bars of optimism you allow. Large κ early, small κ late; κ ≈ 2 is a sane default.

In the figure two slides back, UCB picked x = 0.77 — not the best observed point at 0.66, and not the emptiest region either. It bought the most information per run.

UCB is the easiest to explain. The campaign actually uses qLogNEHVI, which does the same trick in the multi-objective setting; see part three.

Optimising the acquisition is a different kind of problem

1 · FitMaximise the marginal likelihood for kernel hyperparameters. Seconds.
→
2 · Search αMulti-start L-BFGS from 1024 raw samples, 32 restarts. Seconds.
→
3 · SimulateOne CAD → mesh → flow → scalar chain. Minutes to hours.
→

Step 2 is a global optimisation over the analytic surrogate. It is allowed to be greedy, restart everywhere, and evaluate the objective a million times — because that objective is two matrix products, not a solver.

The expensive function is called exactly once per outer iteration. Everything else is arithmetic.

Data efficiency is why this fits CFD at all

MethodEvaluations for a 6-D problem
Full factorial, 5 levels15 625
Genetic algorithm10³ – 10⁴
Adjoint (if available)10¹ – 10², local
Bayesian optimization10¹ – 10², global

At ten minutes per design, 15 625 runs is three months of continuous compute. A hundred runs is an afternoon and an evening.

And in CFD you rarely need to tune the optimiser: stock BoTorch settings — ARD Matérn-5/2, normalised inputs, standardised outputs — already work. The engineering effort belongs in the geometry and the mesh, not in the acquisition function.

This campaign: 3 baselines + 12 screening designs was enough to reach a defensible decision.
Part three

Building it

OpenFOAM, Bayesian optimization, and what actually changed now that agents write the code.

The bottleneck in computational engineering was never typing. It was trust.

An agent will write you a CAD generator, a Snakefile, a GP model and a cluster profile in an afternoon. It will do it confidently whether or not any of it is correct.

So the scarce skill moved. It is no longer knowing the API. It is being able to state, in executable form, what “working” means — and then refusing to believe anything that has not passed.

This is the honest version of "AI writes the code now". The productivity gain is real, and it is entirely conditional on having a verification harness.

Test-driven, from the top down

Write the test that quantifies the result before the code that produces it.

  1. Top-level, physical. “The straight channel must match 12νUL/H² to 15 %.” That test is the study.
  2. Structural. Inlet patch area equals H×depth. Mass imbalance < 10⁻⁵.
  3. Numerical. The outlet metric must be stationary over the last 50 samples, not merely residual-converged.
  4. Unit. Does bo_to_geo respect its interval?

A top-level test forces quantification. You cannot write “the mixer should mix well”; you have to commit to a number, a tolerance and a reference — which is the same discipline a referee will apply later.

In this repository those tests are not prose. They are rules in a Snakefile and standalone validators that any run must pass before an objective is allowed to exist.

If the test lives only in a chat transcript, it does not exist.

validate_mesh_patches.py, validate_flow_balance.py, verify_flow_convergence.py, verify_scalar_convergence.py — each one is a gate wired into the DAG.

What a top-level test caught here

The CAD generator assigned patches by face normal. Every x-normal face of every internal deflector became an inlet or an outlet.

  • “inlet” spanned x = 0…22 mm;
  • “outlet” spanned x = 2…24 mm;
  • inlet area was 2.85× the physical cross-section;
  • velocity and scalar were being injected through obstacle faces.

Every objective from that era is void. An entire 28-design campaign, discarded.

Nothing crashed. Every solver converged. checkMesh said Mesh OK. The Pareto fronts looked plausible and were completely meaningless.

The repair is a test, not a fix: every point of an inlet or outlet face must lie on x = 0 or x = L, checked by reconstructing the polyMesh patches independently of the CAD that made them.

This is why the gates run before an objective is allowed to be written, and why the archived results are kept as provenance and never pooled.

validate_mesh_patches.py reconstructs patch polygons from constant/polyMesh and compares against the CAD manifest — deliberately not trusting the generator that produced both.

Codify the tests in a language that outlives the session

Snakemake

Rules with declared inputs and outputs. The DAG is the specification of what must exist and in which order.

pytest

For the Python layer: parameter transforms, interval arithmetic, config validation.

Assertions in the workflow

Non-negotiables inline: grep -q "Mesh OK", bad-face count, analytic pressure check.

The common property: a machine can re-run all of it without you, on a different host, next year. That is the difference between a verification harness and a memory of having checked.

Separate the writer from the reviewer

Why

An agent that just wrote a function is the worst possible reviewer of it. Its context is full of the reasons the code should work.

  • one session — one model — develops;
  • a clean session, ideally a different model, tests and reports;
  • the reviewer gets the artefacts and the acceptance criteria, not the reasoning.

It works because the failure modes are uncorrelated. Two models rarely hallucinate the same API in the same place, and a fresh context has no sunk cost in the design.

Live example from this very study: a laptop cartesian2DMesh was aborting with free(): invalid pointer. The workflow was blamed first. Only re-running the binary outside the workflow located it.

Report what the run did, not what it was supposed to do.

The cfMesh crash was real: rebuilt from the ESI integration fork instead of SourceForge, the same case meshes in 7 s.

Snakemake: the DAG is the experiment

A rule declares what it needs and what it makes. Snakemake works out the order, what is already done, and what must be redone.

rule hydro_simpleFoam:
    threads: NP
    resources: nodes=1
    input:  ".workflow/hydro_decompose.done"
    output: "pressureDrop.csv"
    shell:  sh("hydro_simpleFoam",
        "{MPI_PREFIX}simpleFoam -parallel | tee log")

Why it earns its place

  • Resumable. Kill it at design 40 of 95; restart continues at 40.
  • Provenance. Change the mesh dict and only the affected rules re-run.
  • Honest failure. A rule either produced its output or it did not.
  • Portable. The same DAG runs on a laptop and on SLURM.
16 rules per design, grouped into three cheap serial groups plus the two MPI solves.

One workflow, any machine — the backend is a profile

--profile profiles/local   # laptop / login node
--profile profiles/local2  # one design at a time
--profile profiles/slurm   # compute nodes

No if cluster: anywhere in the workflow. A different workload manager is a new profile directory, not a fork of the study.

np is one config key and is the single source of truth for both the MPI launcher and numberOfSubdomains — so the launcher can never disagree with the decomposition.

The cost asymmetry is the whole argument

Fit the GP to 100 points
~1 s · login node
Optimise the acquisition
~3 s · login node
One CFD evaluation (np = 2)
~9 min · compute node

The surrogate half of Bayesian optimization never needs the cluster. 100 designs × 6 parameters is a 100×100 matrix. The BO driver runs on one core for the whole campaign; Snakemake sends only the simulations to the queue.

This is why the orchestrator job asks for one core and 4 GB for seven days, and everything expensive is a separate allocation.

Batch BO: available, and often not the point

q > 1 proposes several designs at once and fills a machine. The acquisition already supports it.

But with a budget of ~100 simulations, the sequential loop is usually better, not merely simpler.

  • every design is chosen knowing all previous results;
  • a batch of 8 makes 8 decisions from stale information;
  • you are running other projects on the same allocation anyway.

Use q > 1 when wall-clock is the binding constraint — a deadline, a reservation, an idle machine. Use q = 1 when total simulation count is the binding constraint, which in CFD it nearly always is.

This campaign fixes q = 1 in config and the driver refuses anything else, so the record cannot drift.

Semi-automatic, not fire-and-forget

ARD length scales are a free sensitivity analysis. A parameter with a huge length scale does nothing over its whole range; a tiny one means the objective is twitchy there. You get this after every single evaluation, at no cost.

Acting on it — tightening bounds, dropping a dead parameter, changing the parameterisation — is a human decision, and it is where most of the value is.

One study becomes the prior for the next

When a screen tells you the topology cannot reach the target, the correct move is not more samples. It is a new hypothesis.

P(topology works | 12 designs) ≪ P(topology works)

The twelve runs did not find an optimum. They bought a posterior over study designs — and that posterior said: change the mechanism.

This is Bayes at the level above the optimiser. The prior for the next campaign — which bounds are plausible, which mechanism is needed, what the pressure cost of asymmetry is — is entirely inherited from the one that failed.

A negative result with a predeclared gate is cheap, publishable, and reusable. A negative result without one is just a shrug.

This is exactly how TwoLayerSerpentineCrossingMixer came to exist: the planar mechanism stretches the interface but never multiplies it.

Two objectives: a front, not a winner

With one objective there is a best design. With two there is a set: every design where you cannot improve mixing without paying more pressure.

In d objectives the Pareto set is a (d−1)-dimensional surface in objective space, drawn from a 6-dimensional design space.

How BO targets a surface

Not by scalarising into one number and losing the trade-off. By maximising the hypervolume the front encloses against a fixed reference point.

ref = (20 Pa, Is = 1)

  • qLogNEHVI — expected gain in that volume from the next design, noise-aware, log-scaled for numerical sanity;
  • the reference point is declared in config, because it silently defines what “improvement” means;
  • failed designs are excluded, never given a fictitious penalty.

You sample the surface, then re-simulate the interesting corners at higher fidelity to confirm. The front is a hypothesis until you do.

What CAD-in-the-loop BO actually delivers

Manufacturable by construction

Feature-size constraints live in the parameterisation, so every proposal is a shape that can be etched or printed — not one to be repaired afterwards.

A trade-off, not a point

The output is a front. Choosing on it is an engineering decision with a pump curve and a budget attached.

Auditable

Every design keeps its CAD parameters, its mesh report, its flow balance and its logs. A number without provenance is not a result.

Concretely: microreactors, lab-on-chip, process intensification — devices where geometry is the process, and where a prototype costs weeks.

Nothing failed numerically. The physics simply is not there.

This planar topology stretches and diffuses the inlet interface. It never multiplies it. No setting of the six parameters buys a factor of four in mixing index, and the one design that got closest cost 11.9× the straight-channel pressure.

The full 32 + 80 campaign is blocked in code. Running it would have cost ~19 hours of CFD to confirm what twelve designs already showed.

results/corrected_boundary_v3/screening_gate.json records this decision as data, not prose.

What the twelve runs actually bought

A verified pipeline

CAD, mesh, flow, scalar, objectives and their gates — reproducible on four different machines to the digit.

A quantified mechanism limit

Interface stretching alone tops out near a mixing index of 0.17 at this Re and Sc. That is a number the next study starts from.

A cheaper next question

Not “tune this harder” but “does a three-dimensional crossing topology multiply the interface?” — a different hypothesis, same harness.

The harness is the transferable asset. Swapping the CAD generator and the mesh dictionary points all of this at a new device; the profiles, the gates, the failure classification and the container do not change.
